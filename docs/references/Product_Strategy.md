## Part 2: Design of a New Persuasive AI Technology

### The Purpose and Use

The following is a new persuasive AI technology design I am planning to create, which includes the following purpose: to enable users to remember their good intentions and achieve their desired results by leveraging cues that prompt them to act on time. The inspiration for this came from Todd Rogers and Katherine Milkman's research paper, *Reminders through Association*. In their research findings, they state that reminders-through-association is when a person associates their intentions with a prompt or a cue in an upcoming moment when it can be actioned upon (Rogers & Milkman, 2016). Research indicates that forgetfulness, rather than a lack of willpower, often prevents us from reaching our goals (Rogers & Milkman, 2016).

### The UX Design

The following approach will inform my design decision, as learned in our UX and Society course, Averkiadi (2025): the problem domain (e.g., use context), business goals, relevance to the problem domain, ethical considerations, intended result (e.g., behavior reshape or reinforce), and technology feasibility. The problem domain or use context is to help individuals remember to act upon their intentions when they want or need to. Through studies conducted, reminders-through-association reduced follow-through failures and helped people complete their intentions (Rogers & Milkman, 2016). The more distinct the cue (e.g., reminder) is, the more likely people are to follow through with their intentions, as proven in one unique study by Rogers and Milkman (Rogers & Milkman, 2016). Their study demonstrated a 36% increase in customers carrying out their intentions when reminded with a stuffed alien at a register compared to the control group, which received a text-based message for the intention reminder (Rogers & Milkman, 2016). Both received an intention reminder cue at the beginning of the study to act upon the intention. However, the stuffed alien was almost unforgettable, which is reflected in the significant increase in follow-through.

The business goal is to make an impact on AI for social good and to be the leading business protecting humans in the human-AI interaction world that is prioritizing AI over people. The goal is to leverage AI as a tool to support humans, rather than replace their autonomy and creativity. Ethical considerations are key here, as the persuasive AI technology design will be human-centered and personalized based on past behaviors. It will not overwhelm the user or persuade them in a coercive way that removes the user's autonomy and volition. It has been argued that AI can be used to protect individuals' autonomy through its computational advancements, ensuring that persuasive cues are kept in check to prevent dictation or manipulation of users (Calboli, 2025). If cues or nudges do not consider the user's freedom of choice, they can take away their autonomy (Calboli, 2025). Ethical considerations such as data privacy and transparency will be a top priority. To support that transparency, my design would incorporate Explainable AI (XAI) so that the reasoning behind each cue is inspectable and auditable by the user, rather than a black box — in keeping with the ethical standards Calboli (2025) describes. (XAI makes a system's behavior more interpretable; it does not by itself guarantee complete transparency, particularly for large models, so it is used here as one safeguard among several rather than a blanket guarantee.)

The intended result of my persuasive AI technology design aligns with my business goal of protecting social good for humans in an ever-increasing AI-interaction world. On technical feasibility, my design assigns the right method to each job rather than treating "AI" as a single capability:

- **Timing the cue** — surfacing a reminder close to the moment a user can act — is a context- and pattern-prediction problem. The system learns from the user's own logged behavior and from the on-device context signals available to it (time of day, routine, and, with explicit permission, signals such as location or activity) to time cues as near the intended moment as the mobile platform can reliably deliver. In practice, cues begin as simple, user-defined reminders and become better-timed as the system learns the user's patterns. Precise "to-the-minute" delivery is treated as an aspiration constrained by real mobile-platform limits (background execution limits, battery optimization, and exact-alarm restrictions), not as a guaranteed capability.
- **A large language model (LLM)** is used for *language*, not timing: helping the user craft distinctive, memorable cues and supporting a conversational way to set them up. Using an LLM to *decide when* to remind would be the wrong tool; timing is handled by the prediction approach above.

Within their cue settings, users can review reports of their own past behaviors and successes, which they can use to fine-tune their future cues and interactions with the technology. My design prototype will be based on research and testing (field test) and an evaluation cycle, in alignment with our UX and Society learnings, Averkiadi (2025).

### The MVP and Its Success Metrics

Because the full vision depends on AI capabilities that are not present in the product on day one, the design rolls out in phases. The first phase is a **free, local-only minimum viable product (MVP) that contains no AI**. In it, the user captures an intention, designs a distinctive cue for it, chooses when the cue should surface, and logs whether they acted. It runs entirely on the device — no accounts, no cloud, and no data leaving the phone — establishing the same privacy posture the later, paid, AI-powered version must honor. The MVP's job is to prove that people will create and keep using cue-based reminders *before* any AI is introduced.

Because the MVP collects **no telemetry**, its success is measured only through signals that Google Play Console reports from store- and device-level data, with no in-app tracking.

**Desired directional outcome:** *Increase the number of new users who keep the app through their first 30 days.*

- **North-star metric:** 30-day retention (Retained Installers).
- **Leading indicators:** 7-day retention; early uninstalls; store-listing conversion; early review sentiment.
- **Lagging indicators:** 30-day retention; uninstall-rate trend; average rating and review-sentiment trend.
- **Counter-metrics (guardrails):** spikes in uninstalls; reviews citing nagging or annoyance; rating declines. (Rising "stickiness" is deliberately *not* treated as a success target, since maximizing time-in-app would conflict with the autonomy-first thesis.)

Direct measures of follow-through, engagement depth, and other in-app behavior are intentionally out of scope for the free MVP, because capturing them would require telemetry the privacy posture forbids. Those questions belong to the later, instrumented version.

### Product Strategy

**Target audience.** My primary audience is adults aged 18–50 who actively use modern apps and AI and who set good intentions — health, study, work, personal habits — but repeatedly fail to follow through because they *forget* in the moment, not because they lack willpower (Rogers & Milkman, 2016). Within that group, I am deliberately targeting the privacy- and autonomy-conscious user who is wary of apps that surveil or manipulate them, since that user is both underserved today and aligned with the product's core stance.

**Problem to solve.** Two problems, one stacked on the other. First, the follow-through gap: people forget to act on their intentions when it matters, and generic reminders are easy to ignore, whereas distinctive, associated cues are far more effective (the 36% lift in Rogers & Milkman, 2016). Second, the market gap: most behavior-change and persuasive-tech apps close that gap by maximizing engagement and harvesting data, prioritizing the platform over the person. Little is on offer for someone who wants the behavioral benefit *without* surrendering autonomy or privacy.

**Value proposition.** Help people act on their own intentions at the moment that matters — through distinctive reminders-through-association — while protecting their autonomy and keeping their data on their device. In short: *remember what you meant to do, without being nagged, manipulated, or surveilled.* The future paid tier adds AI that learns better cue timing and content from the user's own local data, with its reasoning kept inspectable.

**Competitive advantage / strategic differentiation.** The wedge is a stance incumbents structurally cannot copy: local-only, privacy-first, and non-coercive by design. Competitors whose revenue depends on data collection and engagement maximization cannot match a "we never take your data, and we want you to need us less" position without undermining their own business model. Reinforcing that wedge: a research-grounded mechanism (cue distinctiveness), transparency (fully user-set logic in the MVP; XAI in the paid tier), and a self-discovery framing that helps users remember what has worked *for them* rather than optimizing them against a pooled model.

**Growth strategy / channel strategy.** Because the product collects no telemetry and runs no data-driven ad attribution, growth leans on channels that fit a privacy-first, values-led product rather than performance marketing: app-store optimization (store-listing conversion is one of the few signals I can actually measure), organic word-of-mouth (the privacy/autonomy stance is the kind of values hook that earns advocacy), positioning and content around behavior change and "AI for social good," and presence in values-aligned communities (privacy, productivity, behavior change). The free MVP is itself the top of the funnel: it builds the trust and retention that later convert to the paid AI tier.

**Business model / monetization strategy.** Freemium, and pointedly *not* data- or ad-driven, because monetizing from data would contradict the entire thesis. The local, no-AI MVP is free and serves as the trust-building entry point. The future AI-powered tier is a paid subscription, where the recurring price maps to the recurring cost and value of the AI layer (learned timing, distinctive-cue generation, inspectable insights). Revenue comes from users choosing to pay for value, never from selling users or their data — which is both the ethical position and a durable differentiator.

**How this informs decision-making and design.** The strategy is not separate from the build; it sets the guardrails for it. Privacy-first dictates the local-only architecture, no accounts, and no telemetry. Autonomy-first means cues are user-controlled, dismissible, and non-coercive — and that rising engagement is treated as a *counter-metric*, not a goal. The no-data monetization model produces the free-MVP-to-paid-AI structure rather than an ad layer. The reminders-through-association evidence makes distinctive cue design the centerpiece of the UX. And the no-telemetry stance is exactly why the MVP's success is defined by retention measured through Play Console, with deeper validation reserved for the instrumented, paid version. Every proposed feature is tested against one question: does it preserve the local, autonomy-respecting posture that is the product's reason to exist?

### The Research Approach

The approach I would use for researching and investigating the UX design of my proposed persuasive AI technology would first start with existing primary research, as I have already started to do. In addition to the resources I have cited above, I would continue to leverage insights from MindShift, an LLM research application similar to my design (Wu et al., 2024). Based on the insights gleaned from this primary research, I plan to conduct my research via Wizard of Oz (human-AI interaction simulated by myself to mimic an AI chatbot interaction) to provide a controlled environment, followed by user interviews. I would create a pre-written script for the user and myself (acting as the AI chatbot) to follow in the Wizard of Oz setting. I would then schedule follow-up user interviews to gain more qualitative insights. To recruit participants for both the Wizard of Oz sessions and the user interviews, I would create a pre-screener survey to ensure I recruit the correct target audience. For the purpose and problem domain of my persuasive AI technology, my target audience would include individuals aged 18–50 who are actively engaging with AI and modern applications. Lastly, I plan to ask participants in the Wizard of Oz study to fill out a post–System Usability Scale (SUS) survey to gain quantitative analysis.

### Societal Impact

There are global privacy, ethical, and risk impacts of using LLMs in persuasive AI technology, given the persuasive messages and cues that may be generated. To address these concerns and provide a safe social impact, I aim to leverage learnings from the MindShift study and to run open-source models that I host or deploy on-device, so that users' data is not sent to a third-party AI provider (Wu et al., 2024). Privacy is protected through two distinct measures: keeping processing local wherever feasible, and encrypting user data at rest and in transit — encryption being a separate safeguard from the choice of model, not a property of the model itself.

I anticipate that my persuasive AI technology may have a positive impact on society, contributing to improved mental and physical health at both personal and community levels, as well as innovation and growth in the professional sector. Another AI engine similar in spirit has shown impact in the health domain through personalized recommendations and nudges: a Graph Neural Network–based recommendation system delivered over a million nudges to more than eighty-four thousand people, who in turn engaged in more physical activity than those who did not receive the nudges (Chiam et al., 2024). That work demonstrates that personalized nudging can change behavior at scale — but it relies on centralized, cross-user data. My approach differs architecturally as well as ethically: it favors local, privacy-preserving processing and is designed to protect autonomy and encourage self-discovery, helping users remember what has worked for them rather than optimizing them against a pooled model. This influences how society views human-AI interaction, fostering trust while preserving human authenticity.
